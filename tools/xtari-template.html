The Xtari Look

Visual and motion language of STRELA-10. Everything below is drawn from the game's own source: the colours and every sprite on this page are read from src/core.js and src/sprites.js.

Xtari is what an Atari 2600 game would look like if the 2600 had a few more colours and a second screen, but the same stubbornness. Hard pixels. A few big shapes. Flat colour. Constant motion. Nothing is soft, nothing fades politely, and every picture is readable at a glance because it has to be.

The rules

1

One pixel is the unit

The world is 256 wide and drawn at integer scale. A sprite pixel is never scaled by a fraction, rotated or blurred. If it does not sit on the grid it does not exist. Text is a 3x5 bitmap font, upper case only, doubled for headings.

2

Small, with a silhouette

Enemies are 3 to 13 pixels wide. At that size the outline is all there is, so each type has its own: a missile is a stack, a bomber is a flat bar with a canopy, a diver is an arrowhead, a phantom is a skull. If you cannot tell two sprites apart in blur, one of them is wrong.

3

Colour is information

Colour means role before it means beauty. Red is the thing that kills a city. Green and blue are ships that carry things. Purple is armoured. Yellow and orange are fire and heavy ordnance. White is a hit, a flash, an event. The palette is fixed at 26 colours, and a sprite uses 3 to 6 of them. The 2600 changed colour per scanline; so do we, by painting a sprite row by row.

4

Everything flickers

Every moving sprite has two frames. A flame, a thruster, a wing, a canopy. They swap 5 to 14 times a second, out of step with each other (each enemy has its own phase), which is how a 2600 made one sprite feel alive. A sprite with one frame is a prop.

5

A black shadow, one pixel, down and right

Every sprite is drawn twice: first all black, offset one pixel down and right, then in colour. That is the whole lighting model. It lifts a sprite off any background, including a cloud, a planet or a mountain, without ever needing a halo or a glow. In cloud, a white outline stands in for it.

6

Trails are dotted, not drawn

Motion is shown with a jagged one-pixel trail: bright near the head, dim and every second pixel at the tail, with a black outline so it reads over bright sky. A trail is also a prediction. It tells the player where a thing has been, and so where it is going.

7

Things break into themselves

When an enemy dies it does not puff. Its own pixels fly apart in its own colours, a short hit-stop freezes the frame (45 ms for a ship, 80 ms for a carrier, 120 ms when a city falls), and a ring of the blast's colour expands. You see what you destroyed.

8

Rhythm is the visual language too

Pulses, blinks and shield cycles are on fixed beats, never random. A shield is up 1.5 s, down 0.85 s, up 0.6 s, down 0.85 s. A phantom is solid for 0.95 s of every 1.5 s, and all phantoms share the beat. A diver hangs for 0.8 s while a dotted line shows its target. If it flashes it is telling you something, and the something is a time.

Palette

26 colours, no more. Black is a colour: it is the shadow, the window, the space between. Nothing is mixed, tinted or alpha-blended except the clouds, the fade-in of a banner, and the dim of a phantom.

Bestiary

Every enemy has a role, a way of acting, and a counter. A new enemy has to bring all three. The first time each pattern appears, the game says so in a one-line tip.

Motion

ThingRule
MissilesFall nose-down, accelerate 35% over the fall, ride the wind (stronger on the lower screen) and bounce off the side walls. They always aim at a city or a turret.
ShipsCross at constant speed, 1.7x a missile's pace. They never change direction or speed. They are the rhythm section.
WeaversSway sideways in a travelling wave. The sway is symmetrical and known in advance, so it nets to nothing: it lands exactly where it was aimed.
DiversCruise, stop dead, flash for 0.8 s with a dotted line down to the target, then dive at 170 px/s. Stopped is the opening; falling is the penalty.
SatellitesThe fastest thing in the game and the only thing that bobs. Yellow, because it is worth a poster.
SpawnAnything deployed by another enemy (bombs, platform missiles, a canopy's three missiles) arrives in a ring of white pixels so you can see where it came from.

Effects

  • Blasts are concentric one-pixel rings that cycle through the rainbow, each ring a different hue. Blast size shows power; chain size shows skill.
  • Shatter: a dying sprite's own pixels, in their own colours, fly outward from the centre. Big ships break into every second pixel.
  • Hit-stop: a freeze of 45 to 120 ms on a significant kill or a city loss. Nothing is shaken at the moment of impact; the stillness is the impact.
  • Flash and shake are for the player's losses, not the enemy's. A city falling flashes the whole screen. A kill never does.
  • Scorch marks: a missile that hits bare ground leaves a dark crescent with a glowing core that cools over six seconds and fades over forty. The ground remembers the wave.
  • Raster bars: bands of colour that change every other scanline, as the 2600's racing-the-beam tricks did, sit behind the wave banner and nowhere else. Use them for announcements, not for scenery.
  • Danger: with two cities left the screen tints red at the edges, a text warning pulses on the lower screen, and a tone repeats every seven seconds.

The screen

Two 256 x 192 screens with a 24 pixel gap, as on a handheld with a hinge. The upper screen is the sky: planets, clouds, distant ships. The lower is the ground: cities, turrets, impact. The gap is part of the world; things fall through it. The HUD is on the upper screen and kept to two rows: wave, score, chain. Menus are written as if the game were already on that handheld: a header plate, buttons with a hard black shadow, a footer plate with the controls.

Agitprop

The voice is eighties Cold War hyperbole, played straight, with us as the good guys. We are the people, the plan and the Motherland. The enemy is capital, and it is always an -ist. Everything is shouted, and everything is a slogan.

The rule: the joke is the tone, never the target. We are earnest, and so is the enemy's name. Nobody real is named, and nobody real is the villain.

Wave structure

A wave is music. It has a motif, an answer, a build, a rest and a finish.

ABAA+BCBA+BA+B+C

A = a missile pattern, B = a ship pattern, C = a wildcard. The first phrase is easy, every third is call and response, the last is the climax and gets the strongest pairing. Under the banner a roster shows which enemies are coming, so the wave can be read before it starts.

  • A pattern is a timeline, not a spawn rate. Where and when every enemy arrives is known.
  • Gaps shrink as difficulty climbs and are cut short when you clear the sky early, so a skilled player is never kept waiting.
  • Late waves layer patterns on top of each other; small drizzle missiles fill the gaps.
  • Lives persist across waves in a campaign and reset only on a new planet. Waves are about reading, not reflexes.

Adding something new

Do

  • Draw it as character art in src/sprites.js, two frames.
  • Give it a silhouette no other enemy has.
  • Give it a rhythm: something that happens on a beat the player can learn.
  • Give it a counter: a moment it is easy to hit.
  • Add it to a pattern, give the pattern a tip, and check the telemetry (tools/balance).

Don't

  • Scale, rotate, blur or glow a sprite.
  • Use a 27th colour or a gradient.
  • Make something that moves randomly. If it is unpredictable it is not fair; if it is not fair it is not Xtari.
  • Add a flash or shake for the enemy's death.
  • Wink at the player. The slogans mean it.
  • Let a pattern work without the player having a choice.
